# Pairs Trading & Statistical Arbitrage in Equities: Literature Notes (for a TSLA–SPCX pair)

Research date: 2026-10-13. Scope: peer-reviewed and working-paper literature on pairs trading / statistical arbitrage in equities, with a view to a pair of two Musk-linked US mega-caps — Tesla (TSLA) and SpaceX (Nasdaq: SPCX, listed 2026-06-12, ≈4 months of history).

**Method & limitations of this note.** All findings below come from web-search result snippets (search engine summaries of abstracts, publisher pages, repository records and secondary write-ups such as CXO Advisory and Quantpedia). Direct full-text fetches (arXiv, SSRN, RePEc, university repositories) were blocked by the sandbox network policy (HTTP 403 on CONNECT), and the per-turn web-search budget was exhausted before every follow-up could be run. Consequently: (i) numbers are reported as the snippet stated them, with the sample period when given; (ii) where a number came from a secondary summary rather than the paper, that is flagged; (iii) several well-known results that I could not re-verify from a retrieved source are listed under *Gaps* rather than asserted. The report writer should treat secondary-summary numbers as "to be checked against the original table".

## Key Question 1 — Canonical papers and methods: how are pairs selected and traded (distance, cointegration, copula, Kalman/time-series, ML), and what exactly did each canonical paper find?

### Takeaway
The field is conventionally mapped (Krauss 2017) into five families — distance, cointegration, time-series (OU/Kalman), stochastic-control and "other" (copula, PCA/factor-residual, ML). The canonical US evidence is: GGR (2006) ≈11% p.a. excess return 1962–2002 for the distance method; Do & Faff (2010, 2012) show the same method decays to ≈0.33%/month in 2003–09 and ≈30 bps/month risk-adjusted net of costs; Rad, Low & Faff (2016) find distance ≈ cointegration (38 vs 33 bps/month net) ≫ copula (5 bps) over 1962–2014; Avellaneda & Lee (2010) report net Sharpe 1.44 (PCA) / 1.1 (ETF) 1997–2007 falling to 0.9 in 2003–07; Chen et al. (2019, Management Science) and Engelberg–Gao–Jagannathan (2009) decompose the profits into short-term reversal + industry momentum and into liquidity provision around news, respectively.

### Cited Findings

**Field map / survey**
- Krauss, C. (2017), "Statistical arbitrage pairs trading strategies: Review and outlook", *Journal of Economic Surveys* 31(2), 513–545 (journal cite reported second-hand; the working-paper version is FAU IWQW Discussion Paper 09/2015). The survey draws on >90 papers and sorts the literature into five groups: **distance, cointegration, time-series, stochastic control, and "other approaches"**, assessing each approach's strengths and weaknesses for research and implementation — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/iwqwdp/092015.html); [FAU working paper PDF](https://www.iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2016/03/09-2015.pdf); [MetaTOC listing](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/73343-statistical-arbitrage-pairs-trading-strategies-review-and-outlook).
- A third-party summary of the survey states that cointegration and stochastic-control methods "often excel in out-of-sample tests" (secondary reading, not the paper's wording) — [tradingstrategy.ai](https://tradingstrategy.ai/docs/learn/papers-stat-arb.html).
- A 2025 University of Warsaw working paper by Yufei Sun surveys non-machine-learning pairs-trading methods published 2016–2023 (follow-up to Krauss) — [WNE UW working paper](https://www.wne.uw.edu.pl/download_file/6095/0).

**Distance method — Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst (GGR)**
- GGR, "Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule", *Review of Financial Studies* (2006): using daily US data 1962–2002, self-financing portfolios of pairs earn **up to 11% average annualized excess returns**; pairs are formed by minimum distance between normalized historical prices; profits "typically exceed conservative transaction-cost estimates"; the authors attribute profits to temporary mispricing of close substitutes and show the effect is distinct from ordinary reversal profits — [NBER w7032 (earlier version)](https://nber.org/papers/w7032); [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2013/06/pairs-trading-kicks-butt/); [SUFE abstract page](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/355449).
- The earlier NBER working-paper version (sample 1962–1997) reported **up to 12%** p.a. for self-financing portfolios of top pairs and noted that part of the profit "may be due to market microstructure effects" — [NBER w7032](https://nber.org/papers/w7032).
- Portfolios of the 20 best-matching pairs had negative returns in only **71 of 474 months**; raw profitability declined after 1988 (one summary: highest profitability in the 1970s–80s, "notable decline after 1989"); public dissemination of the 1998 working paper "has apparently not depressed risk-adjusted returns" (per the paper's own extension to 2002) — [CXO Advisory classic-paper summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/classic-paper-matched-pairs-trading/); [podcast summary](https://podcast.ausha.co/papers-with-backtest-an-algorithmic-trading-journey/exploring-pairs-trading-historical-correlations-and-market-efficiency-insights-for-today-s-algorithmic-traders).
- GGR trading rule as replicated by later work: 12-month formation, 6-month trading, open when the normalized spread diverges by **2 historical standard deviations**, close on convergence or end of trading period (described in Engelberg et al.'s and Fil's replications) — [SSRN 1330689](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1330689); [arXiv 2010.01157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01157v1).

**Distance method decay — Do & Faff**
- Do, B. & Faff, R. (2010), "Does Simple Pairs Trading Still Work?", *Financial Analysts Journal* 66(4), 83–95: confirms "the continuing downward trend in profitability of pairs trading"; the strategy "performs strongly during periods of prolonged turbulence, including the recent global financial crisis"; a "track record" refinement (how often a pair's spread crossed zero historically) raises profits by **22 bps/month for bank stocks** — [CFA Institute FAJ page](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2010/does-simple-pairs-trading-still-work); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v66y2010i4p83-95.html); [Strathclyde record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/41067/).
- Secondary source gives the sub-period figures from Do & Faff (2010): **1.24% mean monthly excess return in 1962–88 vs 0.33% in 2003–09**; the authors identify two candidate explanations — the market-efficiency hypothesis and the arbitrage-risk hypothesis — [Springer 2025 article citing Do & Faff](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-025-00416-0).
- Do & Faff (2012), "Are pairs trading profits robust to trading costs?", *Journal of Financial Research* 35(2), 261–287, US equities **1963–2009**: after commissions, market impact and short-selling fees "pairs trading remains profitable, albeit at much more modest levels"; well-matched pairs formed within refined industry groups earn a risk-adjusted **≈30 bps/month**; the contrarian strategies are "largely unprofitable after 2002" — [Strathclyde record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/41722/); [Western Sydney record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/are-pairs-trading-profits-robust-to-trading-costs/).
- Fil (2020) reports that Do & Faff (2012) split costs into commissions, market impact and short-selling costs, applying a **0.6% p.a. short-selling fee based on D'Avolio (2002)** — [arXiv 2010.01157 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01157).
- Do & Faff (2020, *Accounting & Finance*), "Pairs trading and idiosyncratic cash flow risk": idiosyncratic cash-flow risk — a major holding cost when substitutes are close but imperfect — explains a substantial part of the decline in US pairs-trading profitability since the 1990s — [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/pairs-trading-and-idiosyncratic-cash-flow-risk/).

**Distance vs cointegration vs copula — Rad, Low & Faff (2016)**
- Rad, H., Low, R.K.Y. & Faff, R. (2016), "The profitability of pairs trading strategies: distance, cointegration and copula methods", *Quantitative Finance* 16(10), 1541–1558, DOI 10.1080/14697688.2016.1164337; entire US equity market **1962–2014** with time-varying trading costs. Mean monthly excess returns **before costs: distance 91 bps, cointegration 85 bps, copula 43 bps; after costs: 38, 33 and 5 bps**. Trading opportunities for distance and cointegration "reduced considerably from 2009" while copula frequency stayed stable; all strategies show positive significant alphas after risk factors; cointegration performed best in turbulent markets — [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-profitability-of-pairs-trading-strategies-distance-cointegrat/); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/taf/quantf/v16y2016i10p1541-1558.html).
- CXO Advisory's reading of the same paper: 0.36% and 0.33% vs 0.05% net monthly, "performance peaked ~1990 and declined to unattractive levels post-2000" — [CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/best-stock-pairs-trading-method).

**Cointegration approach — Vidyamurthy; Huck & Afawubo; Clegg**
- Vidyamurthy, G. (2004), *Pairs Trading: Quantitative Methods and Analysis* (Wiley, 224 pp.): Part One covers time series, factor models/APT and Kalman filtering; Part Two uses a **factor model together with cointegration tests to select pairs**, proposes a way to estimate holding times and sets trading bands; a reviewer notes it is "not a ready-to-trade recipe" — [Wiley listing](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/finance-economics-law/pairs-trading-978-0-471-46067-1); [Kwanti review](https://kwanti.com/?p=8).
- Huck, N. & Afawubo, K. (2015), *Applied Economics* 47(6), 599–613, "Pairs trading and selection methods: is cointegration superior?": after controlling for risk and transaction costs, the **distance method generates insignificant excess returns**, whereas cointegration is the superior selection method — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/v47y2015i6p599-613.html); [i-CIRENA record](https://i-cirena.ubfc.fr/node/2078).
- Clegg, M. (2014), "On the Persistence of Cointegration in Pairs Trading" (working paper, Jan 2014): S&P 500 constituents, daily, 2002–2012; with the ADF test an average **5.0% of pairs are cointegrated in any year — about what chance predicts**; of pairs cointegrated in one year, an average of **4.9% were cointegrated the next year** (other parts of the write-up quote 4.5%–7.2% re-cointegration rates); incidence of cointegration was markedly higher in 2008–09 — [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/essential-assumption-of-pairs-trading-wrong). (Secondary summary; figures not reconciled within the page.)

**Time-series / Kalman (OU) approach**
- Elliott, R., van der Hoek, J. & Malcolm, W. (2005), "Pairs trading", *Quantitative Finance* 5(3), 271–276: models the spread as a mean-reverting Gaussian Markov chain (discrete OU) observed in Gaussian noise; a Kalman filter estimates the hidden spread; parameters estimated by an offline smoother (Shumway–Stoffer) or an online EM filter; a commentary notes typos in the online-algorithm equations of the published version — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v5y2005i3p271-276.html); [nmfin.tech note](https://nmfin.tech/?p=6647). No empirical performance figures were retrievable.
- Avellaneda, M. & Lee, J.-H. (2010), "Statistical arbitrage in the U.S. equities market", *Quantitative Finance* 10(7), 761–782: residuals from PCA or sector-ETF regressions are modelled as OU processes. **After transaction costs, PCA-based strategies average Sharpe 1.44 over 1997–2007, with stronger performance before 2003; 2003–2007 average Sharpe only 0.9. ETF-based strategies: Sharpe 1.1 over 1997–2007 with similar degradation since 2002; ETF signals with trading-volume adjustment reach Sharpe 1.51 in 2003–2007.** Results are consistent with Khandani & Lo's "unwinding" theory of the August 2007 quant drawdown — [NYU abstract](https://cims.nyu.edu/ams/abstracts/avellaneda.html); [NYU preprint](https://math.nyu.edu/~avellane/AvellanedaLeeStatArb071008.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v10y2010i7p761-782.html).

**Copula approach**
- Krauss, C. & Stübinger, J. (2017), "Nonlinear dependence modeling with bivariate copulas: statistical arbitrage pairs trading on the S&P 100", *Applied Economics* 49(52), 5352–5369; S&P 100 constituents 1990–2014; t-copulas fitted to all pairs in a formation period; **top-5 mean-reversion pairs 7.98% p.a. out of sample (Sharpe 1.52); top-5 momentum pairs 7.22% p.a. (Sharpe 1.33)** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/v49y2017i52p5352-5369.html); [FAU discussion paper 15/2015](https://www.iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/15-2015.pdf).
- Xie, Liew, Wu & Zou (2016), "Pairs trading with copulas", *Journal of Trading* 11(3), 41–52, and Liew & Wu (2013) "Pairs trading: a copula approach" are the other copula references; only bibliographic entries were retrievable — [Iranian Journal of Financial Research reference list](https://jfr.ut.ac.ir/article_87753_en.html).

**Decompositions of where profits come from**
- Engelberg, J., Gao, P. & Jagannathan, R. (2009 SSRN; Kellogg WP), "An Anatomy of Pairs Trading: The Role of Idiosyncratic News, Common Information and Liquidity": daily prices Jan 1992–Jun 2006 with news/trading data 1993–2005; GGR-style industry-matched pairs, top 200, 2-σ trigger. Profits are higher when divergence stems from news that temporarily reduces one stock's liquidity, or from common news to which one stock reacts faster; lower when divergence is tied to value-relevant idiosyncratic news; stocks with less sell-side coverage/institutional holding respond more sluggishly to common information — [SSRN 1330689](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1330689); [Kellogg page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2009/an-anatomy-of-pairs-trading-the-role-of-idiosyncratic).
- Chen, H., Chen, S., Chen, Z. & Li, F. (2019), "Empirical Investigation of an Equity Pairs Trading Strategy", *Management Science* 65(1), 370–389: pairs strategy earns large significant abnormal returns; the signal decomposes into **short-term reversal and "pairs momentum"**, and pairs momentum is largely explained by one-month industry momentum; controlling for lagged one-month industry return leaves a pairs-minus-industry differential of ≈0.29%/month (value-weighted) — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2017.2825); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v65y2019i1p370-389.html).
- Jacobs, H. & Weber, M. (2015), "On the determinants of pairs trading profitability", *Journal of Financial Markets* 23, 75–97: cross-country study of 34 markets shows abnormal pairs returns are persistent across markets; in US data, "the type of news leading to pair divergence, the dynamics of investor attention as well as the dynamics of limits to arbitrage are important drivers of the strategy's time-varying performance" — [SSRN 2545440](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2545440); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v23y2015icp75-97.html). CXO's summary of the earlier (2012) working paper: pairs trading is roughly **twice as profitable on high-distraction days**, with ≈20% higher re-convergence likelihood and ≈10% higher average return on re-convergence (US, 1962–2008) — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/pairs-trading-and-market-turbulence/).

**Machine-learning approaches**
- Krauss, C., Do, X.A. & Huck, N. (Return figures not retrievable — see Gaps.)
- Huck, N. (2009, 2010), *EJOR*: pairs selection via multiple bivariate return forecasts combined with multicriteria (ELECTRE/outranking) decision techniques, applied to S&P 100, "promising results in terms of excess return and directional forecasting"; the 2010 paper (EJOR 207(3), 1702–1716) extends to multi-step-ahead forecasting — [RePEc 2010](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/ejores/v207y2010i3p1702-1716.html); [PSB page](https://www.faculty-psbedu.paris/fr/node/40653).
- Sarmento, S. & Horta, N. (2020), *Expert Systems with Applications*: OPTICS clustering for pair selection plus forecasting models to shorten drawdowns; **Sharpe 3.79 vs 3.58 and 2.59** for standard approaches, "at the expense of decreasing overall profitability" — [Técnico Lisboa extended abstract](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/281870113705364/ExtendedAbstract.pdf); [journal record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0957-4174/volumes-and-issues/362/paper-detail/15925).
- A KAIST study on KOSPI 100 uses deep learning for spread forecasting and RL for thresholds, motivated by "the profitability of traditional pair trading strategies is decreasing due to the fragile correlation relationship and the declining arbitrage opportunities" — [KAIST repository](https://dspace.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/308768).

### Inferences
- For a *single* pair such as TSLA–SPCX, the distance method's logic (choose the pair with minimum historical price distance from a large universe) is inapplicable — there is no selection step; the relevant literature is the time-series/OU and factor-residual branch (Vidyamurthy, Elliott et al., Avellaneda & Lee), where the pair is given and the question is whether a stationary spread exists and how fast it reverts.
- The decompositions (Chen et al.; Engelberg et al.) imply that a sizeable share of historical pairs profits is short-horizon reversal and liquidity provision after one-sided news — a mechanism that, for two heavily traded mega-caps with dense analyst coverage, the Engelberg et al. results suggest should be weaker (profits are higher when one leg is thinly covered and reacts sluggishly).

- Krauss, Do & Huck (2017) daily return figures (commonly quoted as ≈0.45%/day before costs for the ensemble, deteriorating in later years) could not be verified from any retrieved source — treat as unverified.
- Avellaneda & Lee's estimation details (60-day window, kappa filter requiring mean-reversion time under ~30 days) could not be re-verified from retrieved sources.

## Key Question 2 — How profitable have the strategies been over time, and have profits decayed?

### Takeaway
Every long-sample US study finds decay: GGR's own data show a post-1988 drop; Do & Faff report 1.24%/month (1962–88) → 0.33%/month (2003–09) and "largely unprofitable after 2002" net of costs; Rad–Low–Faff report trading opportunities collapsing from 2009; Avellaneda & Lee's Sharpe fell from ~1.4 to 0.9 after 2002; Fil (2020) finds 1990–2020 distance/cointegration rules do not beat the market benchmark. The consistent exceptions are crisis/bear-market episodes (2008–09, Covid), when pairs profits spike. Recent (2024) replications on 2003–2023 data still show positive Sharpe (~1.35) but with returns largely explained by factor exposure.

### Cited Findings
- GGR (2006): raw profitability decreases for a post-1988 subsample; the 20-pair portfolios had negative returns in only 71 of 474 months (1962–2002) — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/classic-paper-matched-pairs-trading/); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2013/06/pairs-trading-kicks-butt/).
- Do & Faff (2010): continuing downward trend; **1.24%/month (1962–88) vs 0.33%/month (2003–09)**; strong performance in prolonged turbulence incl. the GFC — [CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2010/does-simple-pairs-trading-still-work); [Springer 2025 citing Do & Faff](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-025-00416-0).
- Do & Faff (2012): ≈30 bps/month risk-adjusted net for refined-industry pairs, 1963–2009; "largely unprofitable after 2002" — [Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/41722/).
- Rad, Low & Faff (2016): net 38/33/5 bps per month (distance/cointegration/copula) over 1962–2014; opportunities for distance and cointegration "reduced considerably from 2009"; secondary reading: performance peaked ≈1990 and declined to unattractive levels post-2000 — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-profitability-of-pairs-trading-strategies-distance-cointegrat/); [CXO](https://cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/best-stock-pairs-trading-method).
- Avellaneda & Lee (2010): PCA Sharpe 1.44 (1997–2007) → 0.9 (2003–07); ETF Sharpe 1.1 with degradation since 2002 — [NYU abstract](https://cims.nyu.edu/ams/abstracts/avellaneda.html).
- Fil, M. (2020), "Gold Standard Pairs Trading Rules: Are They Valid?", arXiv:2010.01157 [q-fin.TR]: tests the most common distance and cointegration rules on US equities **1990–2020** including the Covid-19 crisis; "the strategy overall does not beat the market benchmark, even with hyperparameter tuning", but "performs very strongly in bear markets"; optimal parameters are strongly related to market factors; the introduction notes that reasonable transaction costs "often nearly eliminate excess returns in more recent work" (citing Do & Faff 2010, Rad et al. 2015) — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01157v1); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01157).
- Zhu (2024, Yale undergraduate/working paper), replicating GGR on **2003–2023**: distance strategy **Sharpe 1.35 and ≈6.2% average annual excess return**; in a 6-factor model a one-s.d. increase in the momentum factor "can nearly wipe out the return of the best-performing strategy"; returns interpreted as compensation for aggregate risk (not clearly stated whether net of costs) — [Yale PDF](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/Zhu_Pairs_Trading.pdf).
- Hussein (2025, Gothenburg master's thesis, not peer-reviewed), cointegration approach on S&P 500 stocks 2005–2024: Sharpe ratios "well above 2" for high-volume pairs, performance improved in 2015–2024 vs earlier years; commissions affect performance more than borrowing costs — [GUPEA record](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/dc1a29d8-4290-42c7-bf97-ee82eb7251d9).
- A replication of GGR on "the past twenty years" found a compounded annual excess return of 6.2% for the top strategy; a separate study covering 2004–2009 found the algorithm "does not yield consistent positive excess returns" — [harbourfrontquant summary](https://harbourfrontquant.substack.com/p/further-on-the-profitability-of-pairs); [Erasmus thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/8530/Cummins,%20R.L.%20%28299180%29.pdf).
- An unattributed recent review summarised on Substack concludes "pairs trading profitability has weakened over time and become increasingly regime-dependent" — [harbourfrontquant note](https://substack.com/@harbourfrontquant/note/c-262229197) (authorship unverified).
- Attribution of decline: a secondary summary attributes it to competition from quantitative traders — [tradingstrategy.ai](https://tradingstrategy.ai/docs/learn/papers-stat-arb.html); Do & Faff (2010) offer market-efficiency vs arbitrage-risk hypotheses — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-025-00416-0); Do & Faff (2020) point to idiosyncratic cash-flow risk — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/pairs-trading-and-idiosyncratic-cash-flow-risk/).
- Sector context on the mean-reversion stat-arb crowd: the S&P 500 fell 15% Feb–Mar 2001 and a further 21% late Aug–mid Sep 2001, hurting mean-reversion stat-arb funds — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2btgiowdmfyg7ib5snvnk/portfolio/come-together-statistical-arbitrage).

### Inferences
- Decade-by-decade the US evidence reads roughly: 1960s–80s ≈1.2%/month gross (distance); 1990s falling; post-2002 ≈0.3%/month gross and near zero net for simple rules; episodic spikes in 2008–09 and 2020. The 2024 Zhu replication's Sharpe 1.35 for 2003–23 sits above the net-of-cost picture from Do & Faff/Rad et al., so cost treatment and factor loadings are the likely reconciling variables.
- Profits that survive appear to be regime-dependent (bear markets/turbulence) and partly factor-driven (momentum, industry momentum), which argues for factor-neutralising any TSLA–SPCX spread and for expecting most of the P&L to arrive in a few high-dispersion episodes.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study covering 2020–2026 US single-stock pairs with full cost accounting was found; the most recent evidence is a 2024 working paper (Zhu) and 2025 theses.
- The Sun (2025, Warsaw) survey's conclusions could not be retrieved before the search budget ran out.

## Key Question 3 — What sample length / data are needed for reliable Engle–Granger and Johansen tests, and what does the literature say about half-life estimation error in short samples?

### Takeaway
The econometric literature finds Johansen and Engle–Granger tests suffer size distortions and low power in small samples; corrections (Reinsel–Ahn, Bartlett, bootstrap, Cheung–Lai response-surface critical values) help but do not remove the problem, and power depends on the *span* of data rather than sampling frequency. OU mean-reversion speed estimates are biased in finite samples (MLE tends to overstate speed; bias is worst when reversion is slow), so half-lives from ~4 months of daily data should be treated as very noisy point estimates. No source gave a single minimum N; applied pairs studies conventionally use 12-month formation windows.

### Cited Findings
- Johansen test: a Monte Carlo study under GARCH errors finds "size distortion in the different co-integration tests considered", though Johansen "still proves to be powerful ... particularly for large sample when the co-integration innovations are Gaussian" — [Romanian Statistical Review](https://www.revistadestatistica.ro/?p=2351).
- The Johansen trace test adjusted by the **Reinsel & Ahn (1988) finite-sample correction** has the most robust performance when lag length is chosen by information criteria (Österholm, Uppsala) — [Uppsala WP 2003:21](https://swopec.hhs.se/uunewp/abs/uunewp2003_021.htm).
- Lund University study: "under circumstances common in empirical applications, all tests suffer from large size distortions and have low power to detect a false cointegration vector"; Johansen (1991) fares slightly better than a KPSS-based alternative; a **Bartlett correction** "improves to a large extent" the LR test; the asymptotic χ² approximation "is not always a good approximation in small samples", bias greater in smaller samples — [Lund WP 2004:29](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/portalfiles/portal/195339751/WP04_29); [SWoPEc](https://swopec.hhs.se/lunewp/abs/lunewp2004_029.htm).
- Weak-exogeneity tests in cointegrated VARs show "severe size distortions ... in small samples"; a bootstrap LR test is suggested for size correction — [HU Berlin](https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/18452/4088).
- Ignoring level shifts "reduces the tests' sizes to zero and causes an important drop in the small sample power for increasing shift magnitudes" — [HU Berlin](https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/items/64db20a6-71f6-462f-8017-9796e7e23b07/full).
- Cheung & Lai (1993), "Finite-sample sizes of Johansen's likelihood ratio tests for cointegration", *Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics* 55(3), 313–328: Monte Carlo study of finite-sample bias of Johansen LR tests; response-surface approximations of finite-sample critical values; bias driven by sample size, system dimension and lag order; lag selection matters — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/obuest/v55y1993i3p313-28.html); [CityU record](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/finitesample-sizes-of-johansens-likelihood-ratio-tests-for-cointe/).
- Lee & Tse (1996, *Journal of Econometrics*): under GARCH errors, Johansen tests "tend to overreject the null hypothesis of no cointegration, but the problem is generally not very serious" — [UCR PDF](https://faculty.ucr.edu/%7Etaelee/paper/1996_Journal_of_Econometrics.pdf). Toda (1994, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 76(1), 66–79) finds the asymptotic procedures "are not very powerful for sample sizes that are typical for economic time series" — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v76y1994i1p66-79.html).
- Engle–Granger: Kvedaras (2004) reports that at **30 observations**, EG power fell from nearly 100% to about 60% when a 4-lag structure was ignored — [Lietuvos Matematikos Rinkinys](https://www.zurnalai.vu.lt/LMR/article/view/32086). A 2025 arXiv critique simulated two independent random walks with **N = 48** and found EG in levels rejected no-cointegration only 5.3% of the time (near nominal size), while running it on first differences rejected in every replication — [arXiv 2512.21424](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.21424) (illustration, not a general result).
- A 2019 University of Macedonia thesis finds that when both data length and sampling frequency vary, "power depends more on the overall sample length than on frequency" — [UoM repository](https://dspace.lib.uom.gr/handle/2159/22799).
- Half-life / OU speed: Bao, Ullah & Wang find the MLE of the mean-reversion parameter tends to overstate the true value; "the true distribution of the MLE can be severely skewed in finite samples and asymptotic distributions may provide misleading results" — [UCR WP 2014-13](https://webarchive.ucr.edu/economics.ucr.edu/repec/ucr/wpaper/201413.pdf). Yu (SMU) finds the standard bias approximation "does not work satisfactorily when the speed of mean reversion is slow", which is a near-unit-root situation and "empirically realistic for financial time series" — [SMU](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soe_research/1424/). Han & Kordzakhia (2025, arXiv) find bias and MSE of the speed estimate fall as sample size grows, with small speeds and small samples producing negative bias in some settings — [arXiv 2503.23677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.23677).
- Half-life bias carries through from AR coefficient bias (Chalimourdas 2014 thesis); median-unbiased correction raised real-exchange-rate half-life estimates e.g. from 5.78 to 6.58 years (Murray & Papell, cited by Ozer-Balli) — [UoM thesis](https://dspace.lib.uom.gr/handle/2159/16052?locale=en); [AEA paper](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/conference/2010/retrieve.php?pdfid=37).
- Applied convention: GGR-style studies use a 12-month formation and 6-month trading window — [SSRN 1330689](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1330689); Clegg tests persistence using one calendar year of daily data — [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/essential-assumption-of-pairs-trading-wrong).

### Inferences
- With ≈85 trading days of SPCX history (June–Oct 2026), both EG and Johansen tests are in the regime where published Monte Carlo work reports material size distortion and low power; a "cointegrated" verdict at the 5% level from 85 daily observations is roughly as informative as Clegg's base rate (≈5% of random S&P 500 pairs test cointegrated in any year).
- Because power depends on span not frequency, switching to intraday data does not substitute for calendar time; it would mainly add microstructure noise.
- Half-life estimates from such a sample are biased toward *faster* reversion (MLE overstates speed), i.e., the strategy will look more attractive in-sample than it is.

### Gaps
- No source gives a numeric minimum sample size (e.g., "T ≥ 100") for EG/Johansen in pairs trading; Cheung & Lai's response-surface tables were not accessible. Hakkio & Rush (1991) "Cointegration: how short is the long run?" could not be searched (budget exhausted).
- No pairs-trading-specific study on multiple-testing/false-discovery rates when scanning many pairs was found; only the general Bailey–López de Prado and Harvey–Liu literature (see Q4).

## Key Question 4 — Known pitfalls: look-ahead, survivorship, short-sale constraints and borrow cost, transaction costs, "spurious" cointegration driven by a common factor; what controls does the literature recommend?

### Takeaway
(i) Common-factor co-movement: Farago & Hjalmarsson (JFQA) show that if two stocks load on one common factor they can be cointegrated without any tradable equilibrium, and that genuine pairwise cointegration would imply implausibly high Sharpe ratios (>10), so cointegration is "not a likely explanation" for pairs profits; the recommended controls are factor-residual spreads (Avellaneda & Lee PCA/ETF residuals; Vidyamurthy APT), industry/factor restrictions, and random-pair/chance base rates (Clegg). (ii) Costs: Do & Faff (2012) use commissions + market impact + 0.6% p.a. borrow fee (D'Avolio 2002) and find profits fall to ≈30 bps/month and vanish post-2002; Rad et al. show copula profits collapse from 43 to 5 bps net. (iii) Short-sale constraints matter causally (Chu–Hirshleifer–Ma: Reg SHO pilot cut anomaly L/S returns by 72 bps/month, all from the short leg). (iv) Survivorship: Krauss–Do–Huck explicitly remove survivor bias; GGR-style replications use CRSP. (v) Data-snooping: Bailey/López de Prado and Harvey–Liu show that scanning many pairs/parameters without logging all trials inflates in-sample Sharpe.

### Cited Findings

**Spurious / factor-driven cointegration**
- Farago, A. & Hjalmarsson, E., "Stock Price Co-Movement and the Foundations of Pairs Trading", *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*: "under the typical assumption that stock returns only have weak and fairly short-lived serial correlations, cointegration of asset prices would result in extremely profitable pairs trading strategies" — a single pair could reach an annualized **Sharpe above ten**; therefore either cointegration does not hold pairwise or serial dependence must stretch "at least upwards of six months"; empirically "cointegration is not a likely explanation for the profitability of pairs trading strategies using ordinary pairs of stocks" — [Cambridge Core](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/stock-price-comovement-and-the-foundations-of-pairs-trading/5B44CCDDDD09F3F909F50C4B0FB7591A); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/is-equity-pairs-trading-profitable-due-to-cointegration/).
- Quantpedia's reading of the same paper: "if both stocks are driven solely by a single common factor, the two prices could easily be cointegrated"; cointegration is not necessary for pairs trading to work; profits may come from short-lived co-movement driven by shared shocks — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/is-equity-pairs-trading-profitable-due-to-cointegration/).
- A Vaasa thesis on Johansen/EG subsector pairs: "the influence of some common factors drastically declines profitability" and relations "are not stable", so in-sample relationships are hard to evaluate out of sample — [Osuva](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/items/f7f41e58-aab6-4551-973a-0b41d098d3b1/full).
- A Comillas paper on STOXX 600 imposes common-factor and industry restrictions to identify long-run relationships and reports outperformance vs GGR (significant abnormal returns, higher Sharpe) — [Comillas repository](https://repositorio.comillas.edu/rest/bitstreams/100166/retrieve).
- Clegg (2014) provides the chance base rate: ≈5.0% of S&P 500 pairs test cointegrated per year under ADF, "about what chance would predict", with ≈4.9% persisting the next year — [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/essential-assumption-of-pairs-trading-wrong).
- Factor-residual construction (control): Avellaneda & Lee model residuals of stock returns on PCA factors or sector ETFs as OU processes (net Sharpe figures in Q1) — [NYU](https://cims.nyu.edu/ams/abstracts/avellaneda.html); Vidyamurthy pairs a factor model with cointegration tests — [Kwanti](https://kwanti.com/?p=8).
- Chen et al. (2019): pairs profits largely attributable to short-term reversal and one-month industry momentum — a factor-based explanation rather than an equilibrium relation — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2017.2825).

**Transaction costs and borrow fees**
- Do & Faff (2012): commissions + market impact + short-selling fee over 1963–2009; refined-industry pairs ≈30 bps/month risk-adjusted; unprofitable after 2002 — [Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/41722/). Fil (2020) reports the borrow assumption as **0.6% p.a. based on D'Avolio (2002)** — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01157).
- Rad, Low & Faff (2016): time-varying trading costs cut distance/cointegration/copula from 91/85/43 to 38/33/5 bps per month — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-profitability-of-pairs-trading-strategies-distance-cointegrat/).
- GGR (2006): profits "typically exceed conservative transaction-cost estimates"; the NBER version: "short-selling and transactions costs affect the magnitude of the excess returns, pairs trading remains profitable for reasonable assumptions" — [NBER w7032](https://nber.org/papers/w7032).
- Australian daily-data study: after 0.15% one-way costs (no bid-ask), two pairs methods were unprofitable — [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=10685). Global study (Hamburg catalogue): positive returns after costs but "deteriorate significantly at a higher level of transaction costs" — [HCU Hamburg](https://katalog.hcu-hamburg.de/vufind/Search2Record/OLC1978492421).
- D'Avolio, G. (2002), "The market for borrowing stock", *Journal of Financial Economics* 66, 271–306; 18 months (Apr 2000–Sep 2001) of data from a large lending intermediary: only **≈9% of stocks (≈206/day) have loan fees above 1% p.a.**, with "specials" averaging **4.3% p.a.**; the general-collateral borrow cost for a value-weighted portfolio is ≈**25 bps/year** (as summarised by a 2025 NBER paper); issues "potentially impossible to short" are <1% of market value; fewer than 1% of stocks on loan become extremely special (negative rebate) — [Cochrane-hosted PDF](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/davolio_borrowing_stock_JF.pdf); [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f228879.pdf).
- Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018), "Trading Costs" (SSRN 3229719): $1.7tn of live institutional executions, 21 developed markets, 19 years; actual costs "an order of magnitude smaller than previous studies suggest"; model validated against Vanguard S&P 500 and iShares Russell 2000 costs — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3229719); [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsxq9yadiccew11t8idc/portfolio/aqr-investor-worries-over-trading-costs-may-be-overblown). (Large-cap basis-point table not retrieved.)
- Hussein (2025 thesis): commissions impact pairs performance more than borrowing costs — [GUPEA](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/dc1a29d8-4290-42c7-bf97-ee82eb7251d9).

**Short-sale constraints / limits to arbitrage**
- Chu, Hirshleifer & Ma (2020), "The Causal Effect of Limits to Arbitrage on Asset Pricing Anomalies", *Journal of Finance* 75(5), 2631–2672: the Reg SHO pilot (every third Russell 3000 stock by volume, from 2004) **reduced combined anomaly long-short returns by 72 bps/month**, entirely from the short legs (earlier draft: 77 bps) — [NBER w24144](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24144); [SSRN 3092992](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3092992).
- Jacobs & Weber (2015): dynamics of limits to arbitrage are a driver of time-varying pairs performance — [SSRN 2545440](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2545440). Engelberg et al.: sluggish response of low-coverage stocks lowers "the risk of a margin call from further divergence" — [SSRN 1330689](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1330689).
- Ofek & Richardson (2000) on IPO lock-up expiry note that short-selling restrictions limit arbitrage of the anticipated supply shock — [NYU archive](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/27072).

**Survivorship / look-ahead**
- Krauss, Do & Huck (2017) train on all S&P 500 constituents "after elimination of survivor bias" — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v259y2017i2p689-702.html). Clegg uses S&P 500 constituents 2002–2012 — [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/essential-assumption-of-pairs-trading-wrong). (No retrieved source quantifies survivorship bias in pairs returns specifically.)

**Data snooping / multiple testing**
- Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, "Probability of Backtest Overfitting": researchers "must collect the out-of-sample performances of all alternatives" tried — [Bailey PDF](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/overfitting.pdf). Bailey et al. (2014) show a search over millions of variants picks in-sample winners that fail out of sample — [carmamaths](https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf). Harvey & Liu, "Evaluating Trading Strategies": conventional thresholds assume a single test; multiple testing "induces many false discoveries"; remedy is higher Sharpe hurdles or haircuts — [CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/measuring-investment-strategy-snooping-bias/). López de Prado: hold-out/walk-forward do not control for the number of trials — [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/insidiousness-of-overfitting-investment-strategies-via-iterative-backtests).
- Fil (2020): optimal pairs-trading parameters are strongly related to market factors, implying parameter choices tuned in one regime do not transfer — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01157v1).

### Inferences
- For TSLA–SPCX, the Farago–Hjalmarsson logic is the central warning: both stocks plausibly load on a "Musk / retail-growth" factor, so a positive cointegration test is consistent with a common factor and *no* tradable equilibrium. The literature's controls translate to: (1) regress each stock's returns on market, sector (auto/aerospace), momentum and a retail-attention proxy and test the residual spread; (2) compare against a random-pair/chance base rate (≈5% of pairs "cointegrate" by chance per Clegg); (3) require that the spread's implied Sharpe be *plausible* — a very high in-sample Sharpe is itself evidence of spuriousness per Farago–Hjalmarsson.
- Cost assumptions in the literature for US equities are: commissions + market impact (time-varying, falling over time) plus a general-collateral borrow of ≈0.25%–0.6% p.a.; for a freshly listed stock with limited float the borrow is more likely to be "special" (D'Avolio: specials average 4.3% p.a.), which should be modelled explicitly.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source explicitly uses a *random-pair benchmark* as a formal test in a published pairs-trading paper (Clegg's chance base rate is the closest); the report writer should present this as an inference from Clegg plus the multiple-testing literature.
- Frazzini–Israel–Moskowitz basis-point cost estimates for US large caps were not retrieved.
- The exact D'Avolio general-collateral figure is uncertain across sources (one secondary source says 25 bps/yr; another commonly cited figure of 17 bps was not found).
- No study quantifies survivorship bias magnitude in pairs backtests specifically.

## Key Question 5 — Anything specific to pairs involving newly listed stocks, or stocks sharing a controlling person / common retail-investor base (lead-lag among "sibling" firms, related-stock co-movement after an IPO)?

### Takeaway
I found **no academic study of pairs trading or lead-lag effects among sibling companies sharing a founder/CEO** (Musk, Dorsey, etc.), and no study of pairs trading on newly listed stocks. The adjacent literature is: (i) director-interlock/common-leadership co-movement (Burt–Hrdlicka–Harford RFS 2020; EPJ Data Science 2014; Khanna–Thomas Chile) and business-group propping (Bae–Cheon–Kang RFS 2008) — common people/ownership *do* induce idiosyncratic return co-movement; (ii) habitat/category co-movement (Barberis–Shleifer–Wurgler JFE 2005; Kumar–Lee JF 2006; a 2026 arXiv paper on retail-driven excess co-movement in China and the US) — a shared retail clientele produces excess co-movement unrelated to fundamentals; (iii) IPO microstructure (Field–Hanka JF 2001; Ofek–Richardson) — the 180-day lock-up expiry (for SPCX ≈ early December 2026) is a known structural break with −1.5% to −3% abnormal returns and a permanent ≈40% volume increase; (iv) shared-analyst-coverage lead-lag (Ali–Hirshleifer JFE 2020) — connected firms show momentum spillover with 1.68%/month alpha. The only Musk-specific empirical work is event studies on his tweets, where the most rigorous (Cunha–Lobão–Alves 2025) finds no significant price impact.

### Cited Findings

**Common controlling person / interlocks / business groups**
- Burt, Hrdlicka & Harford (*Review of Financial Studies*, 2020) use commonality in idiosyncratic returns of firms linked by a shared director; one director's influence shifts firm value by almost 1% per year; the pattern is not explained by industry or other observable economic links — [Aaron Burt page](https://sites.google.com/site/aaronburt).
- EPJ Data Science (2014, arXiv 1410.6646), >1,500 US firms 2007–2011: fewer degrees of separation in the director-interlock network go with stronger stock-return correlation — [arXiv 1410.6646](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1410.6646).
- Khanna & Thomas (Chile): return synchronicity correlates with shared ownership and equity ties, and more strongly with director interlocks — [Columbia GSB PDF](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/3023/Synchronicity_and%20_Firm_Interlocks_080306_to_post.pdf). A Tehran Stock Exchange study finds equity ties raise synchronicity but common directorships do not — [Financial Research Journal](https://jfr.ut.ac.ir/m/article_71563.html?lang=en) (conflicting with US/Chile).
- Bae, Cheon & Kang (*RFS*, 2008): earnings announcements by one Korean chaebol affiliate move the market value of non-announcing affiliates (positive for increases, negative for decreases), stronger when the controlling shareholder's cash-flow rights are higher ("propping") — [ECGI WP](https://ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/SSRN-id594741.pdf).
- Faccio, Morck & Yavuz (*JFE*, 2021): business-group affiliates' idiosyncratic returns respond less to idiosyncratic commodity shocks than unaffiliated peers — [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/355270). Piotroski, Wong & Zhang study price co-movement of network vs non-network affiliated Chinese firms 2000–2014 — [SIOE PDF](https://extranet.sioe.org/uploads/sioe2018/piotroski_wong_zhang.pdf). Li, Liao, Luo & Zhang (*Pacific-Basin Finance Journal*, 2014): significant co-movement for Chinese firms in the same province, influenced by size and ownership structure — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pacfin/v30y2014icp158-172.html).
- Multi-CEOs: a 2024 *Academy of Management Discoveries* paper (TU Dortmund / HEC Lausanne) studies how Musk, Ghosn, Dorsey and Jobs justified running several companies in parallel — it is about legitimation tactics (symbolic pay, professing abstention from cross-company decisions), **not returns or co-movement** — [TU Dortmund](https://www.tu-dortmund.de/en/detail/how-multi-ceos-justify-their-role-43438). Co-CEO studies (two people, one firm) report outperformance (HBR: 9.5% vs 6.9% annual TSR for 87 firms, or ≈40% higher TSR 1996–2020 in another summary — figures conflict) and are not the same structure — [Livewire](https://www.livewiremarkets.com/wires/few-firms-use-co-ceos-yet-one-study-says-their-share-prices-outperform-by-40).

**Musk-specific empirical work**
- Cunha, Lobão & Alves (2025, *Journal of Behavioral Finance* 26(1), 20–45 per RePEc listing): CAPM and five-factor event study of 33 events from 3,158 Musk tweets over 2,420 trading days concludes Musk's tweets "did not significantly impact Tesla's stock price", interpreted as informational efficiency — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/hbhfxx/v26y2025i1p20-45.html); [Corvinus journal](https://cjssp.uni-corvinus.hu/index.php/cjssp/article/view/977). **Shared retail-investor base / habitat co-movement**
- Barberis, Shleifer & Wurgler (2005), "Comovement", *JFE*: distinguishes fundamentals, category and habitat views; habitat co-movement arises "when a group of investors restricts its trading to a given set of securities, and moves in and out of that set in tandem"; S&P 500 additions/deletions support category/habitat, not fundamentals — [Cochrane-hosted PDF](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/shleifer_comovement_jfe.pdf); [NBER w8895](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8895).
- Kumar & Lee (2006), "Retail Investor Sentiment and Return Comovements", *Journal of Finance* 61(5), 2451–2486: >1.85m retail trades 1991–96; retail investors trade in concert; systematic retail trading explains co-movement mainly in high-retail-concentration stocks (small, value, low-priced) that are costly to arbitrage; **no relationship found for larger firms** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v61y2006i5p2451-2486.html); [NBER conference PDF](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2004/bfs04/lee.pdf).
- A 2026 arXiv paper, "The effect of investor-driven information diffusion on excess comovement: Evidence from retail and institutional investors in China and the United States", tests retail- vs institution-driven excess co-movement; its robustness check excludes same-industry pairs because such stocks "may exhibit greater comovement due to shared factors such as economic cycles, market trends, and industry-specific news" — [arXiv 2605.08726](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08726) (full findings not retrievable).
- Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz, "Attention Induced Trading and Returns: Evidence from Robinhood Users": five-day abnormal returns of **−3% (−6%)** for the top stocks purchased each day (more extreme herding); herding tied to the app's display and attention proxies; commentary reports top-bought stocks fall ≈5% over the next month and extreme herding events reverse ≈9% — [SSRN 3715077 PDF](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/SSRN-id3715077.pdf); [UC Davis commentary](https://aggiehero.ucdavis.edu/curiosity/blog/what-continuing-effect-brokerage-robinhood-stock-market).
- "Active attention, retail investor base, and stock returns" (*J. Behavioral & Experimental Finance* 39, 2023): active retail attention (Robinhood users, Google search) predicts a larger retail base and positive returns over the next four weeks — [EMU repository](https://commons.emich.edu/fac_sch2023/120).
- Option-listing analogue of a listing event reshaping co-movement: newly option-listed stocks increase co-movement with other optioned stocks and decrease with non-optioned stocks, consistent with the habitat view — [Surrey](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-comovement-of-option-listed-stocks/99513739802346).

**Lead-lag among economically/attention-linked firms**
- Ali, U. & Hirshleifer, D. (2020), "Shared Analyst Coverage: Unifying Momentum Spillover Effects", *JFE* 136(3), 649–675: a connected-firm momentum factor based on shared analyst coverage earns **1.68%/month alpha (t = 9.67)**; industry, geography, customer–supplier, segment and technology momentum factors have no alpha once it is controlled for; mechanism is slow analyst updating on linked-firm news, stronger for complex/indirect links; holds internationally — [SSRN 3015582](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3015582); [NBER w25201](https://m.nber.org/papers/w25201). Huang, Lee, Song & Xiang (*JFE*, 2021) find lead-lag patterns across several linkages where investors underreact to continuously arriving lead-firm information — [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/308370).

**Newly listed stocks: lock-up expiry as a structural break**
- Field, L. & Hanka, G. (2001), *Journal of Finance* 56(2), 471–500: 1,948 lock-ups; at expiry a **permanent ≈40% increase in average trading volume and a three-day abnormal return of −1.5%**, larger for venture-backed firms — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i2p471-500.html).
- Ofek & Richardson (2000, NYU WP): lock-up end is "a permanent and large shift in the supply of shares"; despite being fully anticipated, a **1%–3% price drop and 40% volume increase**; attributed to downward-sloping demand with short-sale restrictions limiting arbitrage — [NYU archive](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/27072); [PDF](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/27072/2/wpa99054.pdf).
- Later evidence: a 2021 Erasmus thesis on 2000–2020 finds no significant drop on expiry day but a CAR of −0.94% in the five days prior and permanently higher volume; a 2003–2022 US sample of 2,428 issuers finds negative abnormal returns and higher volume, largest for VC-backed, positive for buyout-backed — [Erasmus thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/60187/Bachelor-Thesis-Final-Version-498076ll.pdf); [USP Revista](https://revistas.usp.br/rcf/article/download/186531/172127/493266). Neuer Markt (172 expirations): significant negative abnormal returns and ≈25% volume increase — [Nowak 2004](https://folia.unifr.ch/documents/317901/files/nowak_AFE_2004.pdf).
- IPO effects on peers: industry competitors experience negative price reactions around IPOs and operating-performance deterioration afterwards (Bhagat-style paper); an Indian study finds significant association between rivals' prices before and after a listing — [Bhagat PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/bhagat/newgamein%20townipos.pdf); [SSHO 2023](https://doaj.org/article/1687c3c3a70248579dba4e00da580f63).

### Inferences
- The closest academic analogues to a TSLA–SPCX pair all point the same way: co-movement induced by a shared person (director interlocks, chaebol propping), a shared clientele (habitat/retail), or shared analyst coverage is *real* but is a **factor/attention phenomenon, not an equilibrium price relation**. In Farago–Hjalmarsson terms, it is exactly the setting where a cointegration test can pass for the wrong reason.
- Kumar & Lee find retail-driven co-movement only in small, hard-to-arbitrage stocks, not large firms; whether that generalises to a mega-cap with an unusually concentrated retail/Musk-fan base is an open empirical question, not something the literature settles.
- Ali–Hirshleifer's shared-coverage lead-lag suggests testing whether TSLA returns lead SPCX (or vice versa) at daily/weekly horizons before assuming contemporaneous spread mean reversion; but their effect is a cross-sectional monthly momentum spillover, not a daily pairs signal.
- SPCX's 180-day lock-up expiry (≈ 9 December 2026 if standard) is a documented supply shock (−1.5% to −3%, +40% volume) that will sit inside any 6-month trading window and can break a spread estimated on the first four months; shorting SPCX into that date also faces the "special" borrow regime D'Avolio documents for constrained stocks.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or working paper on pairs trading, cointegration or lead-lag among companies sharing a founder/CEO (Musk's Tesla/SpaceX/xAI, Dorsey's Twitter/Square, Chinese founder groups) was found; searches on "common CEO", "multi-CEO", "dual CEO" returned only the AMD legitimation study and co-CEO (single-firm) studies.
- No study of pairs trading on stocks with <12 months of history, nor on cointegration testing immediately post-IPO, was found; the lock-up literature is the only post-IPO structural-break evidence retrieved.
- No academic estimate of TSLA–SPCX return correlation or co-movement exists yet (listing is four months old); only news commentary was found.
- The 2026 arXiv paper on retail-driven excess co-movement (2605.08726) could not be fetched, so its magnitudes are unknown.
- Whether the Robinhood herding results (Barber et al.) translate into *pairwise* excess co-movement among "Musk stocks" is untested in the retrieved literature.
