## Key Question 1 — Merger arbitrage: academic evidence (Mitchell & Pulvino 2001; Baker & Savasoglu 2002; Jindra & Walkling 2004; Jetley & Ji 2010), the pre-announcement "rumour" stage, and how desks hedge a stock-for-stock deal with and without an exchange ratio

### Takeaway
Announced-deal merger arbitrage historically earned roughly 4%–10% a year of risk-adjusted excess return with a short-put-like payoff, but spreads compressed by >400 bp after 2002 as capital flooded in. Pre-announcement ("rumour") trading is a different animal: targets of press rumours jump 3%–7% on the rumour day but only about one-third of rumours are followed by a bid and the average rumour-day gain partially reverses within ten days, so outsiders have no documented systematic edge. For Tesla–SpaceX specifically there is no agreement, no exchange ratio, no identified acquirer and no special committee as of early October 2026, so a classical hedged arb position cannot be constructed; what exists is a prediction-market-priced probability (Kalshi ~41%–59% for a signed deal by spring 2027; Polymarket ~32% for an announcement by 30 June 2027) and a relative-value/"pairs" trade.

### Cited Findings

**Mitchell & Pulvino (2001), "Characteristics of Risk and Return in Risk Arbitrage", Journal of Finance 56(6), 2135–2175**
- Sample: 4,750 mergers, 1963–1998 — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i6p2135-2175.html); [Kellogg research page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2001/characteristics-of-risk-and-return-in-risk-arbitrage/?p=1)
- Risk-arbitrage returns are "positively correlated with market returns in severely depreciating markets but uncorrelated with market returns in flat and appreciating markets"; the authors liken the payoff to selling uncovered index put options — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i6p2135-2175.html); [Cochrane-hosted PDF](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/mitchell-pulvino-characteristics-of-risk-and-return-JF.pdf)
- Using a contingent-claims approach and after transaction costs, the paper reports excess returns of four percent per year — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i6p2135-2175.html)
- A secondary summary (Alpha Architect / Swedroe, 2023) states that controlling for non-linearity the merger-arb portfolio earns positive risk-adjusted returns of 10.3% per annum; this figure was not verified against the paper (the 4% figure is the one in the abstract) — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/merger-spread/)
- "If a deal fails, the arbitrageur incurs a loss, usually much greater than the profits obtained if the deal succeeds" — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/merger-spread/)

**Baker & Savasoglu (2002), "Limited Arbitrage in Mergers and Acquisitions", Journal of Financial Economics, 91–115**
- A diversified risk-arbitrage portfolio earned a monthly abnormal return of roughly 0.6%–0.9% over 1981–1996; profits are attributed to limits on arbitrage (arbitrageurs' capacity to bear risk is capped by deal-completion risk and position size); returns rise with an ex-ante measure of completion risk and with target size — [SUFE academic newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/357797); [Wikipedia, Risk arbitrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_arbitrage)
- Secondary (Wikipedia) comparison: Baker & Savasoglu's 1,901-deal sample (1981–1996) had a 22.7% break rate, versus an 8.0% break rate in a 2010 study of 2,182 mergers (1990–2007) — [Wikipedia, Risk arbitrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_arbitrage) (unverified against originals)

**Jindra & Walkling (2004), "Speculation spreads and the market pricing of proposed acquisitions", Journal of Corporate Finance**
- Sample: 362 cash tender offers, 1981–1995 (US). The "speculation spread" is defined as the gap between the bid price and the target's share price at the end of the first trading day after announcement; the average was about 2%, but ranged from 42% below the initial bid price to 30% above it — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041116214200.htm); [Ohio State news](https://news.osu.edu/study-shows-how-takeover-bids-change-stock-prices-of-firms/); [Drexel repository](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Speculation-spreads-and-the-market-pricing/991021881497804721)
- Speculation spreads are "significantly negatively related to the magnitude of price revision and significantly positively related to offer duration" — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041116214200.htm)

**Jetley & Ji (2010), "The Shrinking Merger Arbitrage Spread: Reasons and Implications", Financial Analysts Journal**
- The merger-arbitrage spread fell by more than 400 basis points after 2002; the decline is economically and statistically significant and coincides with lower aggregate returns of merger-arb hedge funds and heavier inflows into them; part of the drop is attributed to more trading in target stocks after announcement, lower transaction costs and shifts in deal risk; the authors suggest some of the decline is likely permanent — [Analysis Group summary](https://analysisgroup.com/link/f5a479d9a8fb4799b20ff4a989d78a50.aspx); [Wikipedia, Risk arbitrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_arbitrage)

**Pre-bid run-ups and rumours**
- Jarrell & Poulsen (1989): 172 successful cash tender offers, 1981–85; concluded there are several legitimate sources of information allowing investors to anticipate takeover announcements (e.g., 13D filings when investors cross 5%); found only weak evidence that pre-bid run-ups substitute for post-bid mark-ups — [Bank of Canada FSR, King 2012](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2012/01/fsr-1204-king.pdf)
- Schwert (1996), "Markup Pricing in Mergers and Acquisitions": the pre-bid run-up and the post-announcement increase are generally uncorrelated, so the run-up is an added cost to the bidder — [NBER w4863](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4863.pdf); [Bank of Canada FSR](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2012/01/fsr-1204-king.pdf)
- Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn, "Markup Pricing Revisited": a one-dollar increase in the run-up raises the initial offer price by $0.80; bidder gains are decreasing in offer-price markups but increasing in run-ups — [ECGI working paper](https://ecgi.global/working-paper/markup-pricing-revisited); [ECGI PDF](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/finalbettoneckbothorburn.pdf). Betton, Eckbo, Thompson & Thorburn, "Merger Negotiations with Stock Market Feedback": large-sample tests "support rational deal anticipation in runups while strongly rejecting the costly feedback loop" — [Harvard Law Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/02/18/merger-negotiations-with-stock-market-feedback/)
- Ahern & Sosyura (2015), "Rumor Has It: Sensationalism in Financial Media", Review of Financial Studies: sample of 501 merger rumours, 2000–2011; only 167 were followed by a public bid; targets of accurate rumours gained about 6.7% on the rumour date vs about 3.0% for inaccurate ones; for the average firm returns reversed by about −1.4% over the following ten days, "consistent with limited attention"; accuracy tracks journalist experience, specialised education and industry expertise, while less accurate stories use vague wording and focus on well-known firms; investors "do not fully account for the predictive power of these characteristics" — [NBER conference paper PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f72667/f72667.pdf); [Talking Biz News summary](https://talkingbiznews.com/media-moves/rumors-accuracy-and-the-ma-beat/)

**How desks hedge stock-for-stock deals**
- In a fixed-ratio deal the arbitrageur buys the target and shorts the acquirer at the announced ratio; shorting the acquirer isolates the deal spread and hedges acquirer price moves; ratios are usually fractional so the hedge is rarely 1:1 — [IBKR Knowledge Base](https://ibkb.interactivebrokers.com/de/node/2132); [IB Interview Questions, exchange-ratio analysis](https://ibinterviewquestions.com/guides/valuation-investment-banking/exchange-ratio-analysis-stock-for-stock-deals)
- The spread "positively correlates to the perceived risk that the deal will not be consummated at its original terms"; it is also influenced by differences in dividends received vs owed over the deal's life and by difficulty borrowing the acquirer's shares — [IBKR Knowledge Base](https://ibkb.interactivebrokers.com/de/node/2132)
- Collar deals: the exchange ratio floats with the acquirer's price inside a band (illustrative example: ratio fixed at 0.65x while the acquirer trades between $70 and $90), so the hedge becomes a delta hedge of the acquirer ("Collar stock merger, buy target, delta hedge acquirer stock") and arbs use option-based models to value the position — [Princeton Eco467 slides (Brunnermeier, based on Coval & Stafford)](https://www.princeton.edu/~markus/teaching/Eco467/08Lecture/07aMergerArbitrage_slides2008.pdf); [AIMA merger-arbitrage paper (2007)](https://www.aima.org/asset/676DA5D6-8CE4-42D7-A2C6171EAC6382DC)
- Geczy, Musto & Reed (2002), "Stocks are Special Too", JFE: "The effect of short-selling frictions is strongest in merger arbitrage"; "Acquirers' stock is expensive to borrow, especially when the acquirer is small, though the major influence on trading profits is not through expense but availability" — [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/d071c291-2b0e-4dba-93b4-2432b0252006); [Knowledge@Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/equity-loans-how-to-sell-what-you-do-not-own/)

### Inferences
- With no acquirer, no ratio and no agreement, a desk cannot run the textbook long-target/short-acquirer hedge; a position today is a "pre-deal" relative-value bet on the eventual ratio, carrying both direction risk in TSLA and SPCX and the ~40%–70% chance (per prediction markets) that no deal is signed in the window. The literature (Ahern & Sosyura; Schwert) implies the rumour-day pop is partially reversed and that the pre-announcement run-up is not systematically captured by outsiders.
- If a deal were announced as stock-for-stock, Jetley & Ji's post-2002 spread compression and Mitchell & Pulvino's option-like payoff imply a tight spread with fat-tailed regulatory/governance break risk (Delaware related-party scrutiny, Musk's 85%/20% voting asymmetry). Short-side borrow on the acquirer (whichever entity) matters per Geczy–Musto–Reed; SPCX's constrained float until December 2026 makes it the more expensive leg to short.
- A merger announcement would also interact with the lockup and index events below: an announced ratio would turn the December lockup supply into a hedging flow (locked holders could hedge via the other leg), and S&P 500 eligibility of the combined entity would depend on which entity survives (Tesla is already an S&P 500 member).

## Key Question 2 — IPO lockup expiry: abnormal returns and volume (Field & Hanka 2001; Bradley, Jordan, Roten & Yi 2001; Ofek & Richardson 2000; Brav & Gompers 2003; recent work), anticipation, scaling with fraction unlocked and VC/insider ownership, realistic edge after borrow, and SPCX's lockup structure

### Takeaway
The canonical US evidence (1988–1997 samples) is a −1% to −3% abnormal return in a 3-day window around the unlock with a permanent ~40% volume increase, concentrated in VC-backed, high-run-up, high-volume firms; the effect is anticipated in the sense that the date is public, yet it still shows up, and Ofek & Richardson argued it is not exploitable after costs. Recent US samples (2003–2023) show the effect has shrunk to roughly −0.7% over three days. Mega-IPO unlocks are idiosyncratic (Facebook +12.6% on its 777m-share unlock day; Uber −8.7% intraday to a record low; Rivian about −13%; Arm +2.1% because only 9.5% was floated). SPCX's lockup is a staggered "ladder" (20% after Q2 earnings, 7% tranches at days 70/90/105/120/135, 28% after Q3 earnings, remainder at day 180 ≈ 8–9 December 2026) with Musk and significant investors locked 366 days to ~12 June 2027; the August 6 unlock saw a dip to ~$105 and a +2.6% close.

### Cited Findings

**Academic literature**
- Field & Hanka (2001), "The Expiration of IPO Share Lockups", Journal of Finance 56(2), 471–500: 1,948 lockup agreements (1988–1997); a permanent 40% increase in average trading volume at expiration and a three-day abnormal return of −1.5%; "little selling by insiders" while lockups are in effect; abnormal return and volume are "much larger" for VC-financed firms and venture capitalists sell more aggressively than executives and other shareholders; the authors find limited support for several hypotheses but no complete explanation — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i2p471-500.html); [Nowak, AFE 2004 (citing)](https://folia.unifr.ch/documents/317901/files/nowak_AFE_2004.pdf)
- Bradley, Jordan, Yi & Roten (2001), "Venture Capital and IPO Lockup Expiration: An Empirical Analysis", Journal of Financial Research 24(4), 465–493: 2,529 firms, 1988–1997; lockup expirations coincide on average with significant negative abnormal returns, concentrated in VC-backed firms; within VC-backed firms the biggest losses are in high-tech firms, firms with the largest post-IPO run-ups, heaviest volume around expiration, and highest-quality underwriters; CARs stay negative even when volume falls after unlock day, which a later review reads as ruling out the downward-sloping-demand explanation — [UKM repository record](https://ptsldigital.ukm.my/handle/123456789/666812); [Erasmus thesis (citing)](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/67687/572140.pdf)
- Ofek & Richardson (2000), "The IPO Lock-Up Period: Implications for Market Efficiency and Downward Sloping Demand Curves", NYU Stern WP FIN-99-054: "even though the event is totally anticipated, there is a 1%–3% drop in the stock price, and a 40% increase in volume, when the lock-up ends"; "convincing evidence is provided which shows that this inefficiency is not exploitable, i.e., arbitrage is not violated"; evidence "points to a downward sloping demand curve for shares, with the most likely explanation pointing to a permanent, long-run effect" — [NYU Archive PDF](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/27072/2/wpa99054.pdf); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fth/nystfi/99-054.html)
- Brav & Gompers (2003), "The Role of Lockups in Initial Public Offerings", Review of Financial Studies 16(1), 1–29: 2,794 IPOs; results support the commitment (moral-hazard) hypothesis over signalling or underwriter rent extraction; insiders of firms with greater moral-hazard potential lock up longer; insiders of firms with larger excess returns, VC backing or high-quality underwriters are more likely to be released early — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i1p1-29.html); [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/355641)
- Geczy, Musto & Reed (2002), JFE: IPO shares are lent from early on; "underperformance around lockup expiration remains significant even for IPOs that are cheap and easy to borrow"; most IPOs are loaned on their first settlement days — [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/d071c291-2b0e-4dba-93b4-2432b0252006). A secondary summary states IPOs are initially expensive to short (3% fees on average) falling to about 1% over the first six months as lockups expire — [Knowledge@Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/equity-loans-how-to-sell-what-you-do-not-own/) (fee figures not verified against the paper)
- Gibbs & Hao (2018), Journal of Banking & Finance 88, 30–43: daily short selling increases before the lockup expiration date and declines afterward, with higher levels in VC- and PE-backed IPOs — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v88y2018icp30-43.html)
- Liquidity explanation: an EFA-2009 paper argues changes in liquidity after the unlock date account for much of the price reaction and can predict later abnormal returns — [USQ repository](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/9z7y2/can-liquidity-shifts-explain-the-lockup-expiration-effect-in-stock-returns)
- Recent samples (student theses, not peer-reviewed): US 2004–2023, 1,914 lockups: about −0.7% three-day abnormal return, volume peaks on day one and stays ~40% higher — [Aalto bachelor's thesis 2024](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/094e1d37-2114-4480-b6a2-54416f60a25f/full); US 2003–2022: significant negative abnormal returns, VC-backed issuers most negative, buyout-backed issuers show a significant positive return, first study of market-sentiment effects — [Aalto master's thesis](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/05aae052-991d-4947-8363-6b2a5df9fb89/full); Nordic IPOs 2012–2021: negative abnormal return up to 1.61%, more negative for shareholder lock-ups than management lock-ups — [CBS student project](https://research.cbs.dk/en/studentProjects/abnormalities-surrounding-nordic-ipo-lock-up-expiration-an-event-/)
- Damodaran (Dec 2019) on lockups: a few stocks "rise, sometimes sharply, when feared insider selling fails to materialize"; the academic average is small and negative and "averages hide wide variation between stocks" — [Damodaran Substack](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/much-ado-about-liquidity-lockup-expirations-12-11-19)

- Day-count inference: anchoring at the 11 June 2026 prospectus date, day 70 ≈ 20 Aug, day 90 ≈ 9 Sep, day 105 ≈ 24 Sep, day 120 ≈ 9 Oct (matches the reported 9 Oct tranche), day 135 ≈ 24 Oct (reported as "October 24/26" after weekend adjustment), day 180 ≈ 8 Dec (9 Dec if counted from first trade). - Because SPCX's 180-day date is the smallest remaining tranche of the ladder (≈17% of the pool) and the prior tranches passed without a crash (Aug 6: −~5% intraday low to +2.6% close), the literature's "anticipated but still −1% to −3%" pattern suggests the December cliff is less of a supply shock than a conventional 180-day cliff; the post-Q3-earnings 28% tranche (early/mid-November) is roughly four times larger than the October tranches and is the bigger supply event. The Arm precedent shows that when the controlling holder (SoftBank 90%; here Musk + significant investors on 366 days) is excluded, the unlock day can be a non-event.
- Tradable edge: Ofek & Richardson's "not exploitable" conclusion plus Geczy–Musto–Reed's finding that IPO borrow starts near 3% and that underperformance persists even in easy-to-borrow IPOs imply the −0.7% to −1.5% average is roughly the size of a few weeks of borrow plus slippage; Gibbs & Hao show shorts crowd in before the date, which is when borrow is tightest. Buyout-backed issuers showing positive unlock returns and Facebook/Arm rising on the day underline that the sign is not reliable for a single mega-cap.
- Tesla's ~19.0m shares (≈ $3.1bn at $165) are a trivial fraction of the ≈ 4.56bn-share early-release pool; Tesla's own December restriction expiry is immaterial as supply but matters for Tesla's fair-value P&L (the $238m marketability discount unwinds) and as a merger-optics item.

## Key Question 3 — Index inclusion: Nasdaq-100 fast entry and S&P 500 eligibility as applied to SPCX in 2026, other index families, and the literature on the size and timing of the inclusion premium (Shleifer 1986; Harris & Gurel 1986; Chen, Noronha & Singal 2004; Petajisto 2011; Greenwood & Sammon 2025)

### Takeaway
Nasdaq's Fast Entry rule (effective 1 May 2026; top-40 market-cap test; added after the 15th trading day) put SPCX into the Nasdaq-100 on 7 July 2026, and FTSE Russell, MSCI and CRSP also added it within weeks of listing; S&P DJI, by contrast, rejected its own consultation on 4 June 2026 and kept the 12-month seasoning, GAAP-profitability and IWF requirements, so the earliest S&P 500 entry is mid-2027 and SpaceX currently fails the earnings test (Q2 2026 net loss $541m; trailing four quarters > $8bn loss). **Academic literature on the inclusion premium**
- Shleifer (1986), "Do Demand Curves for Stocks Slope Down?": firms added to the S&P 500 between 1976 and 1983 rose 2.79%, argued to be permanent and consistent with downward-sloping demand — [UBB Studia Oeconomica review](https://studia.reviste.ubbcluj.ro/index.php/subboeconomica/article/download/5533/5236/10319); subsequent evidence on permanence is "mixed" — [NBER w8654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8654.pdf)

- Chen, Noronha & Singal (2004), Journal of Finance: "a permanent increase in the price of added firms but no permanent decline for deleted firms", "at odds with extant explanations ... that imply a symmetric price response" — [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361234)
- Petajisto (2011), "The index premium and its hidden cost for index funds", Journal of Empirical Finance 18, 271–288: 1990–2005; the announcement-to-effective-date price move averaged +8.8% for S&P 500 additions and +4.7% for Russell 2000 additions; deletions fell 15.1% and 4.6%; the "index turnover cost" lower bound is 21–28 bp/yr for the S&P 500 and 38–77 bp/yr for the Russell 2000; premia grew over time, peaked in 2000 and declined afterward — [Petajisto PDF](https://ww.petajisto.net/papers/petajisto%202011%20jef%20-%20hidden%20cost%20for%20index%20funds.pdf); [Morningstar UK](https://morningstar.co.uk/uk/news/68110/the-hidden-costs-of-indexing.aspx)
