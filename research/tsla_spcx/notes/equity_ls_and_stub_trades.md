## Key Question 1 — Under which desk/strategy would a TSLA–SPCX trade sit in the standard prime-brokerage / index-provider taxonomy, and what would that desk require before putting it on?

### Takeaway
Every major classifier (HFR, Preqin, Goldman's own fund-of-funds taxonomy) uses four top-level families — Equity Hedge/Long-Short, Event Driven, Relative Value, Macro (plus Multi-Strategy / FoF). A TSLA–SPCX position is *not* a Relative Value stub (there is no mechanically linked claim: Tesla's 19.0m SPCX shares are ~0.2% of Tesla's market cap). It belongs either in **Event Driven – Special Situations / pre-announcement merger speculation** (if the thesis is a Musk-driven combination) or in **Equity Hedge – Fundamental / Sector-specialist L/S** (if the thesis is relative valuation of two Musk "physical AI" platforms). An event-driven desk would require an announced deal or at least definable terms (exchange ratio, structure, regulatory path) before treating it as merger arbitrage; until then it is a directional/thematic pair trade carrying full idiosyncratic and "Musk-factor" risk.

**Merger arbitrage mechanics (what the event-driven desk needs)**
- Merger arbitrage "attempt[s] to profit by speculating on whether announced M&A deals will in fact be successfully completed"; cash deal → long target; stock deal → long target / short acquirer; return profile asymmetric ("larger downside in the case of a deal's failure than the upside offered when a deal succeeds"). — [UBS Event-Driven Strategies](https://www.ubs.com/content/dam/WealthManagementAmericas/documents/event-driven-strategies.pdf); [Fidelity Canada](https://www.fidelity.ca/en/insights/articles/mergerarbitragestrategy/)
- Special situations (Investopedia definition quoted in secondary source): a security bought because of a particular circumstance rather than fundamentals — spin-off, tender offer, M&A, bankruptcy, distress, litigation, capital-structure dislocation, activism. — [Become a Better Investor](https://becomeabetterinvestor.net/?p=16453)

### Inferences
- Because no exchange ratio, structure or signed agreement exists (see Q4), a TSLA–SPCX position fails the entry test for Merger Arbitrage ("announced deals"). It would be booked as *Special Situations / soft-catalyst* or as a fundamental sector (autos–space–AI) pair in Equity Hedge. Both carry beta and crowding risk; HFR's own Special Situations index losing −4.7% in a quarter "as soft catalyst trades in crowded names lost ground" is exactly the risk profile.
- A Relative Value desk would not classify it: RV requires "two closely related securities" with a contractual or balance-sheet link; a 0.2% cross-holding does not create one (see Q3).
- Typical exposure context: fundamental L/S books run ~213% gross (GS, 2025) with net ~50% (MS, 2018); a pair trade sized against that book is a small idiosyncratic sleeve, not a core hedge.

## Key Question 2 — Realised returns and risks of the classic stub / twin-share trades; how long the mispricings persisted; which limits-to-arbitrage explain persistence

### Takeaway
The canonical cases show mispricings that were large (3Com stub −$63/share ≈ −$22bn; Royal Dutch/Shell deviations of up to ~30% with a 9.5% mean absolute deviation 1980–95) and persistent (months for Palm/3Com; decades for Royal Dutch/Shell, only ended by the 2005 unification). Mitchell–Pulvino–Stafford's 82 negative-stub cases took on average 236 days to converge (range 1–2,796), 30% never converged because the parent–subsidiary link was severed first, and the path was rough enough that a smooth path would have raised returns 50%. Explanations: short-sale constraints / lending fees (Palm), noise-trader & local-market sentiment (Royal Dutch/Shell), information costs and fundamental "link-severed" risk (MPS), synchronization risk (Abreu–Brunnermeier), and funding risk (LTCM's forced unwind of the Royal Dutch/Shell trade in 1998).

### Cited Findings

**Lamont & Thaler (2003, JPE) — 3Com / Palm**
- After Palm's March 2000 IPO, "the stub value of 3Com, representing all non-Palm assets and businesses, was estimated to be negative $63, a total of negative $22 billion." — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/can-market-add-and-subtract)
- 3Com retained about 95% of Palm and announced it would distribute those shares by year-end at about 1.5 Palm shares per 3Com share. — [NBER Reporter, Asset Pricing program report](https://www.nber.org/reporter/winter-2004/5/program-report-asset-pricing)
- The mispricing was visible in the financial press immediately yet "persisted for months"; the authors examined six other carve-outs followed by spin-offs with similar persistent negative stubs. — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/can-market-add-and-subtract); [Cochrane review notes](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/cochrane_fall_2004_ap_review.pdf)
- Why arbitrage failed: shorting Palm was too costly or impossible; buying Palm puts failed because put-call parity broke down ("the option market became delinked from the stock market"). — [NBER Reporter](https://www.nber.org/reporter/winter-2004/5/program-report-asset-pricing); [JPE paper PDF (Cochrane site)](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/lamont-and-thaler-add-and-subtract-jpe.pdf)
- Palm's public float was approximately 4% of the company, so the WSJ at the time judged the gap "unlikely to be closed before 3Com completes the spinoff." — [WSJ Interactive via UConn](https://users.business.uconn.edu/jgolec/invwsj/6palm.htm)
- Timeline: 3Com said in May 2000 it would distribute remaining Palm shares as a tax-free dividend on July 27 (record date July 11); a shareholder post dated 2000-07-28 reports the actual ratio was 1.4832 Palm per 3Com share. — [RCR Wireless](https://rcrwireless.com/?p=112059); [Fund Manager forum post](https://www.fundmanagersoftware.com/q2q201/messages/2950.html)
- Rebuttal literature: Cherkes, Jones & Spatt argue "lending fees and spin-off uncertainty are crucially important to understanding the relative levels and co-movement of Palm and 3Com share prices," with a sharp change in relative price behavior once spinoff uncertainty was resolved after trading on 2000-05-08; Cherkes (2024) argues a rational model with spinoff uncertainty plus shorting constraints fits, noting 3Com's own Palm shares could not be lent out. — [Cherkes-Jones-Spatt (EPFL)](https://memento.epfl.ch/public/upload/files/Paper_Spatt.pdf); [Cherkes 2024 (Critical Finance Review forthcoming)](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/forthcoming/papers/cherkes2024solution.pdf)
- Companion article: Lamont & Thaler, "Anomalies: The Law of One Price in Financial Markets," JEP Fall 2003. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jecper/v17y2003i4p191-202.html)

**Mitchell, Pulvino & Stafford (2002, JF) — "Limited Arbitrage in Equity Markets"**
- Sample: 82 negative-stub-value situations 1985–2000 (parent market value below value of its stake in a listed subsidiary), typically after carve-outs or partial acquisitions; trade = long parent / short subsidiary. — [AQR summary](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Limited-Arbitrage-in-Equity-Markets); [Kellogg research page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2002/limited-arbitrage-in-equity-markets/)
- "For 30% of the sample, the link between the parent and its subsidiary is severed before the relative value discrepancy is corrected" (bankruptcy, third-party acquisition, delisting) — i.e., negative stubs are not risk-free. — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2002/limited-arbitrage-in-equity-markets/)
- Time to convergence: average 236 days, range 1–2,796 days (lecture summary of the paper); "substantial variability in the time to termination, even for negative-stub-value investments that eventually converge." — [USI Lugano lecture notes 2026](https://people.lu.usi.ch/franzonf/limitsofarbitrage%202026.pdf)
- "Returns to a specialized arbitrageur would be 50% larger if the path to convergence was smooth rather than as observed." The biggest friction "appears to be the costs associated with imperfect information." — [SUFE academic newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361417)
- German replication: Schuhmacher (2005), "The Parent Company Puzzle on the German Stock Market," finds mispricings "exist and persist, because of costs associated with imperfect information." — [Alexandria/UniSG](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/id/eprint/12199)

**Royal Dutch / Shell twin shares — Rosenthal & Young (1990), Froot & Dabora (1999)**
- Rosenthal & Young, "The seemingly anomalous price behavior of Royal Dutch/Shell and Unilever N.V./PLC," JFE 26(1), 123–141, July 1990. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/r/eee/jfinec/v26y1990i1p123-141.html)
- Froot & Dabora, "How are stock prices affected by the location of trade?", JFE 53(2), 189–216 (1999); sample period 1980–1995. — [Uni Duisburg-Essen literature review page](https://hecf.wiwi.uni-due.de/studium/abschlussarbeiten/abgeschlossene-arbeiten/the-price-efficiency-of-twin-stocks-a-literature-review-7636/); [Scruggs, EFM 2006 "Siamese twins"](https://efmaefm.org/0EFMSYMPOSIUM/2006/papers/07-EFM06%20-SCRUGGS-SIAMESE.pdf)
- Size of deviations: in the early 1980s Royal Dutch traded at a discount of approximately 30% to Shell Transport & Trading. — [Wikipedia: Dual-listed company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-listed_company)
- RBA (2003): mean absolute deviation from parity for Royal Dutch/Shell about 9.5% over 1980–1995 and about 4.7% from September 1999 to 2002. — [RBA RDP 2003-06](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2003/2003-06/examples-of-dual-listed-companies-and-puzzles-in-their-pricing.html)
- Mechanism: "the ratio is highly variable, moving with the markets where the securities are intensively traded"; "the Royal Dutch/Shell relative price moves positively with the Netherlands and U.S. markets and negatively with the U.K. market." — [HBS case via Bookshare](https://bookshare.org/browse/book/1321510)
- Resolution: unification into Royal Dutch Shell plc completed 2005-07-20 — i.e., the twin-share deviation was ended by corporate action, not by arbitrage. — [STOXX notice](https://www.stoxx.com/documents/stoxxnet/Documents/News/2005/July/Unification%20of%20Royal%20Dutch%20Petroleum%20and%20Shell%20Transport%20&%20Trading.pdf); [Shell share unification](https://www.shell.com/investors/information-for-shareholders/share-information/share-unification-information.html)
- LTCM (1998): short Royal Dutch / long Shell, betting the premium would shrink (Shell historically at an 18% discount); position reported at $2.3 billion (secondary source); the position "further shredded LTCM's assets when the shares failed to converge." LTCM lost $4.6 billion in under four months in 1998. — [Wikipedia LTCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-Term_Capital_Management); [Motley Fool 2013](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/09/23/when-wall-streets-dream-team-failed.aspx)

**Theory of persistence**
- Abreu & Brunnermeier (2002, JFE), "Synchronization risk and delayed arbitrage": arbitrageurs learn of mispricing at different times and bear holding costs, so they wait; synchronization risk is distinct from noise-trader and fundamental risk; behavioral price effects "can persist in the short and intermediate run." — [Princeton](https://markus.scholar.princeton.edu/document/154); [Princeton summary page](https://www.princeton.edu/~markus/research/papers/synchronization_risk.htm)
- Abreu & Brunnermeier (2003, Econometrica), "Bubbles and Crashes": inability to coordinate selling lets a bubble survive "for a substantial period" even with rational traders. — [Princeton](https://www.princeton.edu/~markus/research/papers/bubbles_crashes.htm)

### Inferences
- Realised P&L of the textbook trades was poor-to-disastrous for anyone with finite horizon or leverage: the 3Com stub only normalised when the spin-off date/ratio was fixed (May–July 2000), the Royal Dutch/Shell gap outlived LTCM and only closed with the 2005 unification, and MPS show a 30% "never converges" tail. The lesson for TSLA–SPCX: even a *contractually* linked pair can stay dislocated for years; a pair linked only by a common CEO has no convergence mechanism at all unless a deal is announced.
- The three frictions most relevant to SPCX today are (a) borrow cost / float (SPCX IPO'd June 2026 with lockups still rolling off — see Q3), (b) local/"habitat" sentiment (SPCX trades as an AI/space retail favourite; Froot–Dabora show habitat can move a twin's relative price by double digits), and (c) synchronization risk (no catalyst date).

## Key Question 3 — Is a 0.2% cross-holding ever the basis of a stub trade? What threshold do practitioners use? (plus holdco-discount cases)

### Takeaway
No. In the literature a "stub" is defined only when the parent's listed stake is a large fraction of — or exceeds — the parent's own market value (MPS select cases where stake value > parent market cap, i.e., >100%; 3Com held ~95% of Palm; Yahoo's Alibaba + Yahoo Japan stakes exceeded its $40bn market value; Naspers/Prosus and SoftBank trade at 25–60% discounts to NAV dominated by one listed stake). Tesla's 18,990,195 SPCX shares are worth ~$3.0–3.1bn, about 0.2% of Tesla's ~$1.46T market cap — two to three orders of magnitude below anything the stub/holdco literature treats as tradable. Any TSLA–SPCX "relative value" thesis must therefore rest on merger optionality or common-factor exposure, not on sum-of-parts arithmetic.

## Key Question 5 — What does the academic literature say about co-movement of stocks connected through common management or common investor base, and about "connected stocks" return predictability?

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence exists for co-movement via *common ownership* (Anton & Polk 2014: shared active mutual-fund ownership forecasts excess correlation and a cross-stock reversal strategy earning >9%/yr) and via *category/habitat* membership (Barberis, Shleifer & Wurgler 2005: S&P 500 inclusion raises beta/R² with no fundamental change), plus network evidence that *interlocking directorates* raise return correlation (EPJ Data Science 2014) and that investors under-react to director connections (Shi 2025). There is **no peer-reviewed study** on common-CEO/common-founder pairs (Musk: Tesla/SolarCity; Dorsey: Twitter/Square); only journalistic correlation estimates (Tesla–SolarCity 40.6%, Benzinga 2015; "Dorsey discount," Fortune 2016) and student theses on Musk tweets.

### Cited Findings
- **Anton & Polk, "Connected Stocks," JF 2014**: degree of shared active mutual-fund ownership forecasts cross-sectional variation in return correlation after controlling for systematic factors, style, sector and other pair characteristics; the 2003 mutual-fund trading scandal is used as a natural experiment showing shared ownership *causes* excess co-movement; U.S. data 1980–2008, larger stocks, typical pair has ~9 funds in common; a cross-stock reversal strategy earns "in excess of nine percent per year"; long-short hedge-fund index returns covary negatively with the strategy, suggesting hedge funds "may exacerbate this excess comovement." — [LSE paper PDF](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/ConnectedStocks.pdf); [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/37374); [IESE Insight summary](https://www.iese.edu/insight/articles/connect-stocks-improve-trading); [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/360042)
- **Barberis, Shleifer & Wurgler, "Comovement," JFE 75(2), 283–317 (2005)**: after S&P 500 inclusion a stock's beta and R² with the index rise while its beta with non-index stocks falls; rise is larger in bivariate regressions controlling for non-S&P returns; results support "category" and "habitat" views, not the fundamentals view. — [NBER w8895](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8895.pdf); [Shleifer publications page](https://shleifer.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/comovement); [NYU archive](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/26617)
- Follow-up: Coakley et al. (2022) find the BSW beta increase is significant for up to one year but not beyond, and option-implied betas rise less than historical betas post-inclusion. — [UEA eprints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/document/107498)
- **Interlocking directorates** (EPJ Data Science 2014; arXiv 1410.6646): ~1,500 US listed firms, 2007–2011 — "the fewer degrees of separation between two corporations in the interlocking network, the stronger the temporal correlation between their stock price returns." — [EPJ Data Science](https://epjds.epj.org/articles/epjdata/abs/2014/01/13688_2014_Article_30/13688_2014_Article_30.html); [arXiv](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1410.6646)
- **Shi (Columbia JMP, 2025)**: using exogenous directorship shocks, firms mirror interlocked peers' dividend initiations, large dividend increases, equity issuances and splits; a long-short portfolio on this earns abnormal returns, consistent with investor under-reaction to director connections. — [Columbia working paper](https://econ.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2025/09/Job_market_paper_latest.pdf)
- **Eldar, Nili & Pinnington (ECGI)**: higher common ownership by hedge funds is associated with more board interlocks; Big-Three common ownership is not. — [ECGI](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/Paper%3A%20Common%20ownership%20directors%2C%20Ofer%20Eldar%2C%20Yaron%20Nili%2C%20James%20Pinnington.pdf)
- **Tesla–SolarCity (pre-2016 acquisition), journalistic**: Motley Fool (2013-12-02) noted the two were "highly correlated despite operating in different businesses"; Benzinga (April 2015) measured a 40.6% correlation, higher than some oil-linked names, and observed SolarCity rising with Tesla after a Musk tweet with no direct SolarCity relevance. — [Motley Fool 2013](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/12/02/why-do-solarcity-and-tesla-move-together.aspx); [Benzinga 2015](https://feeds2.benzinga.com/general/education/15/04/5373935/solarcity-and-tesla-have-closer-correlation-than-some-oil-stocks-do-); [Quartz on Musk–SolarCity entanglements](https://qz.com/713045/here-are-all-of-elon-musks-entanglements-with-solarcity)
- **Dorsey — Twitter/Square**: Fortune (2016-01-08) observed Square fell ~13.8% in a week roughly matching Twitter and suggested investors priced a "Dorsey discount"; but over Dorsey's tenure Twitter rose ~79% vs Nasdaq-100 +276% while Square rose ~23-fold since its 2015 IPO; Twitter rose on Dorsey's Nov-2021 exit while Square/Block fell 12% to $186.75 — i.e., episodic, not systematic, co-movement. — [Fortune 2016](https://www.fortune.com/2016/01/08/twitter-stock-low); [BNN Bloomberg 2021](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/twitter-jumps-on-dorsey-exit-stock-lagged-indices-in-his-tenure-1.1688494); [Motley Fool 2021-12-03](https://www.fool.com/investing/2021/12/03/why-twitter-is-tanking-this-week)

### Inferences
- The literature predicts TSLA and SPCX *will* co-move in excess of fundamentals through two sourced channels: (i) a shared active-owner base (Baron funds hold SPCX per NPORT filings; retail/"Musk complex" habitat) à la Anton–Polk / BSW, and (ii) formal linkage (framework agreement, related-party supply, overlapping governance) à la the interlock studies. Anton–Polk's result that this excess co-movement *reverses* (cross-stock reversal >9%/yr) implies the tradable edge, if any, is short-horizon mean-reversion of relative moves driven by common-owner flows — a stat-arb/quant L/S construct, not a convergence trade.
- The Tesla–SolarCity precedent is instructive: a sibling pair with 40.6% correlation and heavy Musk entanglement ultimately resolved via a related-party all-stock acquisition (2016) — the same path the sell side now sketches for TSLA–SPCX — so the historical "sibling" payoff was deal-driven, not valuation convergence.
